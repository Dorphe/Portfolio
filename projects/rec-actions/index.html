MaintainX

AI Recommended Actions

Maintenance technicians often work from incomplete data. I designed an AI recommendation engine inside work orders to reduce guesswork and surface historical knowledge when it matters most.

The problem

Work orders arrive with minimal documentation. Technicians frequently encounter key pain points leading to high MTTR (mean time to repair) and rising operational costs.

  • Searching through physical manuals or old reports.
  • Running standard procedures when a known fault is the likely cause.
  • Chasing down supervisors for missing context.

The solution

Recommended actions appear when a technician opens a repair work order.

Key Features

1
Historical pattern recognition Flags whether this machine or similar models have failed this way before.
2
Trustworthy sources Extracts the relevant information from the manual. No more searching a 200-page PDF.
3
Peer insights Surfaces comments from previous interventions on similar issues.

The hypothesis

Synthesize work order history, comments, and manuals into AI-recommended actions delivered directly within the work order. Identify recurring faults across similar machines and surface the fix before the technician starts troubleshooting.

Tying design to ROI

I built a feedback loop to track the effectiveness of each recommendation against three KPIs:

Metric Definition AI Impact Goal
MTTR Mean Time To Repair Decrease by cutting diagnostic guesswork.
MTBF Mean Time Between Failures Increase by suggesting the right long-term fix over a quick patch.
Asset Downtime Total time a machine is non-functional Minimize by getting machines back to Ready faster.

This feedback loop compounds value in three ways

1

Retrains the model based on recommendation outcomes.

2

Exposes successes to technicians and managers, building trust.

3

Creates direct ROI evidence for business cases.

Key design decisions

From “black box” to “open book”

Technicians are field experts. If an AI says “Replace Part X” with no reasoning, they’ll ignore it.

I designed evidence markers into every recommendation. The card shows what it suggests and why: “Based on 3 similar faults in the last 6 months and Page 42 of the Service Manual.”

Human-in-the-loop feedback

I added a single-tap Accept/Dismiss action to each recommendation. Dismissals prompt a quick-select reason list.

Technicians stay in control, and the data science team gets the ground truth labels needed to retrain the model.

Communicating confidence

Showing “82% confidence” felt arbitrary to someone under a machine. I replaced percentage scores with qualitative tiers: “Primary Suggestion” and “Alternative Considerations.”

Technicians naturally think in terms of Plan A and Plan B. The UI was designed to match that.

Source linking

Technicians don’t trust AI summaries of technical text. In maintenance, hallucinations can be dangerous. Every recommendation links directly to the source PDF, opened to the exact page.

Reflections

1
Trust comes first Technicians are skeptical of AI. Every recommendation had to show why it was made.
2
Data quality drives everything The model is only as good as its context. This led to adjacent work: making it effortless for technicians to document their interventions.
