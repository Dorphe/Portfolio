Ubisoft

Oasis

Stories in video games were traditionally written like a movie or a book — linearly. With role playing games featuring player choice dominating the market, Ubisoft had to adapt. Due to Oasis's status as proprietary software and a competitive advantage, this case study won't contain visuals of the product.

The objective

Empower writers and narrative designers to create modular, non-linear stories.

Responsibilities

  • Product design lead
  • Design system manager
  • Assisted with UX research & testing

Chapter 1 — What is non-linear storytelling?

Prior to this iteration of Oasis, writers had been writing the same way they'd write for non-interactive media — like a TV script, with a scene, actors, lines, everything you'd expect from a script.

Simply put, a non-linear narrative is when the story reacts to player input, whether through gameplay or dialogue choices. A player might choose to antagonize a character, which leads to a confrontation or an enemy instead of the character joining the player's party. This can have rippling effects on the story down the line, such as losing alignment with other characters or acquiring different rewards.

Ultimately, the concept is about giving the player agency and letting them shape the narrative with how they play.

The structural shift

Since the narrative is based on player input, a non-linear script looks more like a flowchart or a diagram than a traditional script. The first major change comes from needing to componentize writing. Instead of writing a scene from start to finish, writers need to isolate dialogue line by line so that they can flow into one another depending on what's happening in the game. Lines connect to form a scene. Scenes contain all possible permutations of a dialogue tree. Finally, scenes can be embedded into other scenes to form the larger narrative.

Chapter 2 — A paradigm shift for writers

One of the main challenges was that writers and narrative designers aren't necessarily tech savvy folks. The discipline had been done one way since forever, and non-linear storytelling meant fundamentally shaking up how writers and narrative designers work.

Writer This one's easy: they write the words.
Narrative designer They outline story beats. They're the ones who think about the narrative pacing of the game, how missions flow into one another. They place the boxes the writer's words go into.

Easing users into this new way of working wasn't going to be easy, and the internal tools department was going to need design help.

Chapter 3 — The state of design at the time

The internal tools department at Ubisoft has a very strong engineering-led culture. At the time of my hire, there were 3 UX researchers and I was the first design hire. With very low churn, that means there were roughly 150 extremely skilled .NET developers who had never worked with a designer before. This wasn't your average design-immature organization.

I'm a firm believer in the human factor as the biggest force multiplier in work. Since I was hired specifically for the biggest shift in Ubisoft's tooling strategy, I was determined to do it right.

Embedding into the team

At first, the dynamic was more one of a consultant. I very quickly demanded to be embedded into the development team — it was important to establish ourselves as teammates working toward a common goal. Considering the culture at the time, it would've been easy to only be seen as a pixel pusher. Even though they didn't have much experience with this type of cross-functional collaboration, the team was very open to this new way of working.

Proving value through results

The best way to seal the deal is through the work. Prior to our arrival, there was a direction that had lines embedded into a traditional script structure. There had been some vague feedback, but we put a test plan together and gathered real, actionable data. The team was blown away and we continued validating further hypotheses and eventually ended up with the node-based editor that the tool is today.

Scaling the design team

This successful collaboration eventually made its way through the sinewy hallways of the old textiles factory that is the Ubisoft office, and more and more development teams started asking for our help. The design team at the time of my hire was 4 strong. At the end of my 3 years tenure, the team had scaled to a 20-person organisation and we were well on our way to establishing a design system to be shared by both desktop and web tools.

"This success was a testament to what I, again, think is the most important factor of success in product development: people. When you create an environment where people get along and trust each other, you're 100x likelier to move fast and get to the right conclusions."

Conclusion

Oasis was a success both organisationally, but also on the business end. Ubisoft's shift to a non-linear strategy for its role-playing games paid off, propelling the Assassin's Creed franchise to 200M total sales.
