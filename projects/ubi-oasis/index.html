Ubisoft

Oasis

Video game stories were traditionally written linearly, like a screenplay. As RPGs with player choice grew dominant, Ubisoft needed a new scriptwriting tool. Due to Oasis's proprietary status, this case study contains no product visuals.

The objective

Empower writers and narrative designers to create modular, non-linear stories.

Responsibilities

  • Product design lead
  • Design system manager
  • Assisted with UX research & testing

Chapter 1 — What is non-linear storytelling?

Before Oasis, writers worked the same way they would for film or TV: a scene, actors, lines, top to bottom.

A non-linear narrative reacts to player input. A player might antagonize a character, triggering a confrontation instead of an alliance. That choice ripples through the story: different companions, different rewards, different endings.

The concept is about giving players agency over how the story unfolds.

The structural shift

A non-linear script looks more like a flowchart than a screenplay. Writers isolate dialogue line by line so lines can flow into one another based on game state. Lines connect to form scenes. Scenes contain all permutations of a dialogue tree. Scenes embed into other scenes to form the larger narrative.

Chapter 2 — A paradigm shift for writers

Writers and narrative designers aren't necessarily technical. They'd been working the same way for decades, and non-linear storytelling required a fundamental shift in process.

Writer They write the dialogue, descriptions, and in-game text.
Narrative designer They outline story beats, structure mission flow, and define where player choices branch the narrative. They build the containers the writer's words go into.

Easing both roles into this new way of working required design help from the internal tools department.

Chapter 3 — The state of design at the time

Ubisoft's internal tools department is engineering-led. When I joined, there were 3 UX researchers and no designers. The team was roughly 150 .NET developers, none of whom had worked with a designer before.

I was hired specifically for the biggest shift in Ubisoft's tooling strategy, and getting the team dynamics right was going to matter as much as the product itself.

Embedding into the team

The initial dynamic was consultative. I pushed to be embedded into the development team to establish a real working relationship. Given the culture, there was a risk of being seen as a pixel pusher. Despite having no experience with cross-functional design collaboration, the team was open to it.

Proving value through results

Before our involvement, the tool embedded lines into a traditional script structure. There had been vague feedback but no structured validation. We put a test plan together and gathered actionable data. The results shifted the team's perspective, and we continued validating hypotheses until we arrived at the node-based editor the tool is today.

Scaling the design team

Word spread. More development teams started requesting design support. When I joined, the design team was 4 people. By the end of my 3-year tenure, it had grown to 20, and we were building a shared design system for both desktop and web tools.

Conclusion

Oasis was a success on both fronts: organizational and commercial. Ubisoft's shift to non-linear storytelling helped propel the Assassin's Creed franchise to 200M total sales.
